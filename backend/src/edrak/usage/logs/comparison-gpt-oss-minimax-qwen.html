  • qwen's failures were structural, not incidental.   • The paced run found the real shape of the ceiling. Groq reports 8k tokens per minute per account, but the 429 bodies split it into 7,000 input + 1,000 output. A prompt above 7k input is refused, and so is a request whose expected reply exceeds the whole 1,000-token output minute ("Request too large â€¦ Requested 1327") â€” even after the window rolls. Pacing only manages the remainder; it cannot admit an oversized request. Market's 12 failures were a mix of both classes: a transient overshoot ("Used 799, Requested 202. The provider names max_tokens as the lever, but a probe with a 16,384-token ceiling was admitted on a short prompt, so its estimate follows the prompt's expected reply rather than the ceiling alone. Only a rerun can show whether market recovers from 16/28 and metrics from 37/113.

verdict

  • MiniMax M3.1 Flash is the most reliable model ever run through this pipeline: 98% call success, zero structural failures, every worker completed. It is also the only model that produced a full brief with 47 findings and 45 verified, on the same prompts gpt-oss and qwen used.
  • It is not the cheapest host. $0.3899 per report vs gpt-oss's $0.1383 (2.8أ—) and $0.00248 vs $0.00109 per successful call (2.3أ—), driven almost entirely by output verbosity, not retries. It is also ~10أ— slower per call and ~4.5أ— slower wall-clock.
  • Evidence quality is real but not perfect: 45 of 47 findings were evidence-backed; the brief carries evidence_status: partial, 32 information gaps, and 354 sources. gpt-oss's report was shorter; qwen's seven findings were duplicated and off-topic.
  • The promo changes the accounting, not the verdict. At $0 actual billing MiniMax is free today, but the published-rate comparison above is what a post-promo decision must use â€” and at those rates gpt-oss is still the better default.
  • Pacing made qwen complete, not competitive. With a per-credential token bucket and a 12k-character competitor batch, the run finished: 153 calls, 61 successful (40%), 8 findings verified, 347 sources, a brief in 91 minutes for $0.1629 at published rates. The 413 class disappeared entirely. What remains is depth: 8 findings against MiniMax's 47, two workers effectively empty, coverage 29%, evidence partial.
  • qwen's binding constraint is per-request, not per-minute. On Groq's free tier a request must fit inside one minute on its own: â‰¤7k input tokens, and an expected reply â‰¤1k output tokens as the provider estimates it. Waiting never admits a request that is too large by itself; the levers are a lower reply ceiling at the call site and smaller prompts. Pacing alone cannot buy depth on this tier â€” and where it does apply, the pacer's fixed 600-token output reserve is still the wrong number.
  • Recommended split: keep gpt-oss:120b as the default host (fast, cheap, 74% call success with retries); escalate to MiniMax M3.1 Flash when depth matters more than 70 minutes and $0.40 â€” it is the best available quality-per-run. qwen is viable only as a free-tier fallback where 8 findings and 90 minutes are acceptable, and only with the pacing, competitor chunking and reply ceilings in place.
  • Portable lessons: output verbosity, not input size, is the cost lever at these rates; the metrics stage can consume half a run's budget when verification accepts many findings; and a model can be structurally perfect while still being 3أ— more expensive than the incumbent.
